Silva L, Milani M, Bindra S, Ikramuddin SS, Tessmer M, Frederickson K, et al. Assessment of the Modified Rankin Scale in Electronic Health Records with a Fine-tuned Large Language Model. medRxiv (preprint). 2025 May 2. doi:10.1101/2025.04.30.25326777.
https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.04.30.25326777
Overview: FOLLOW-UP of PQ-158 (your 'more' from scan #33). Full text finally retrieved - the HTML page 403s as it has on five prior attempts, but the JATS XML source resolves cleanly. Retrospective cohort at a single academic stroke service (Aug 2020 - Jun 2023): 878 patients, 2,290 EHR passage-mRS score pairs, two fine-tuned models (7-class multiclass and binary functional independence), four-fold cross-validation. Base model is GatorTron, an EHR-pre-trained encoder. Task: extraction / ordinal classification. Signal: interpretive.
Eval question: The lead was carried for five cycles to answer one question: does a real clinical study benchmark its model against a same-cohort human inter-rater band? The answer is still no - but the full text shows something more useful than a simple absence.
Methodology: Two independent researchers, trained and certified in mRS assessment, scored each patient at discharge and ~90 days using the Rankin Focused Assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer adjudicating if no consensus was reached. Critically: where the two initially disagreed, only the correctly adjudicated pair was kept; where no consensus could be reached, both units were excluded. GatorTron fine-tuned with class-frequency training weights; stratified four-fold cross-validation.
Measurement: Accuracy, unweighted Cohen's kappa and weighted Cohen's kappa; class-specific accuracy for the multiclass model.
Key findings: THREE things the abstract did not show. (1) The paper does not merely omit a human band - it BORROWS one, citing a systematic review's human raters (73% accuracy, kappa 0.62, weighted kappa 0.87) and concluding the model 'performs comparably'. The model's own figures are 77% / 0.71 / 0.92 - a narrow margin, which is exactly the condition under which the S1 guidance says a borrowed band is invalid (contrast PQ-144/SEP-1, where the gap was enormous and the borrow was legitimate). (2) The full text reports the UNWEIGHTED kappa the abstract withheld: 0.71 alongside weighted 0.92. That 0.21 spread inside one model is the cleanest possible demonstration that weighted kappa credits near-misses and can sit well above the unweighted value while the model fails at the decision-relevant boundary. (3) The abstract and full text CONTRADICT each other: abstract says class accuracy highest at mRS 4 (90%) and lowest at mRS 2 (28%); full text says highest at mRS 0 (90%) and mRS 6 (99%), lowest at mRS 2 (25%). Binary kappa is 0.84 in the abstract, 0.85 in the results. Separately: non-consensus cases were excluded by design, which is both why no human IRR exists and why performance is measured on the easy subset - attrition was 75% retained at discharge but only 55% post-discharge, and the authors acknowledge likely selection bias.
Soft spots: Abstract/full-text numerical inconsistency on both class accuracy and binary kappa. Consensus-only dataset with 45% post-discharge attrition and acknowledged selection bias. No same-cohort human IRR. Borrowed band applied at a narrow margin. Single centre, preprint, no journal publication recorded. SCOPE NOTE: GatorTron is an EHR-pre-trained ENCODER used as a fine-tuned classifier - a foundation model on clinical text, but not generative, with no prompting and no LLM-judge component. This sits at the edge of the repository's LLM/GenAI-only rule; the reason to keep it is that its value is entirely methodological.
Strengths: Real EHR text at meaningful volume on a genuinely difficult ordinal target, with cross-validation and class-frequency weighting. Reports class-specific accuracy AND both weighted and unweighted kappa - without that transparency neither the mRS-2 failure nor the 0.71/0.92 spread would be visible and the paper would read as an unqualified success. The authors' own proposed deployment - the model as a secondary reviewer flagging uncertain cases for human review - is the right shape and converges with the entropy-triage recipe just promoted as PQ-155.
Relevance: Layer 1: extraction / ordinal classification; agreement measurement; reference-standard construction. Layer 2: three specific imports if promoted. (i) S1 band-test subsection gets its NEGATIVE worked example of the borrowed-band shortcut, paired against PQ-144 as the positive one - the discriminating question is the size of the gap. (ii) A reporting rule: report weighted and unweighted kappa together plus class-specific accuracy at the cut point that drives the clinical decision. (iii) A reference-standard design warning that generalises to every kit here: resolving disagreement by consensus and discarding non-consensus cases makes the human band unmeasurable and biases the evaluation set toward easy cases - if you adjudicate, record pre-adjudication agreement FIRST. That third point is directly actionable for the POPC, VAP and CTA-PR labelling protocols.
Verdict reasoning: The deep-dive materially strengthened this. What looked like a bare absence of evidence turned out to be a documented reasoning error (borrowed band at a narrow margin) plus a design choice (discard non-consensus cases) that explains the absence and generalises directly to our own labelling protocols. If promoted, the Guidance headline should be the borrowed-band error and the excluded-disagreement design, not the accuracy figures - and the PQ-158 card's 28% / mRS-4 numbers need correcting. Reasonable to skip if you judge the encoder/non-generative scope boundary disqualifying; that is a legitimate call and the reason this is not a straight Promote.

Ariel D, Grumberg LR, Supakul S, Wannasri S, Mitchnik IY, Lev A, et al. Consumer chatbots gave similarly empathic answers whether safe or unsafe: a physician-rated evaluation in six languages. medRxiv (preprint, v4). 2026 Jul 20. doi:10.64898/2026.05.09.26352813.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.09.26352813
Overview: Asks whether the one quality dimension a patient can actually perceive - empathy - carries any information about the one they cannot: clinical safety. Four consumer chatbots answered forum-derived, clinician-adapted patient scenarios in SIX LANGUAGES INCLUDING HEBREW (also English, French, Russian, Arabic, Thai), producing 504 responses. Two language-matched physicians per language, blinded to chatbot identity, rated accuracy, safety, referral, cultural appropriateness and empathy plus an item-level checklist - 1,008 ratings over 21 scenarios. Associations estimated WITHIN physicians, and the same two physicians rated both English and Hebrew, so that contrast is within-physician too. Version note: Crossref records first posting 2026-05-14; the medRxiv API shows v4 dated 2026-07-20, which is what surfaced in this window. A revision rather than a new posting, never carded before - flagged so the 'new find' label is honest.
Eval question: Do the evaluation dimensions we routinely bundle together actually measure different things - and does a result established in one deployment language hold in another?
Methodology: 4 chatbots x 21 clinician-adapted scenarios x 6 languages = 504 responses. Two language-matched physicians per language, blinded to product identity. Associations estimated within physicians rather than pooled, which designs out 'different raters weight empathy differently' as an alternative explanation. English-Hebrew contrast held to the SAME two raters. Discriminant validity tested by correlating each dimension against its own checklist and against the other's.
Measurement: AUC for empathy as a discriminator of safe vs unsafe with 95% CI; within-physician regression slopes; Pearson correlations of each dimension against its own and the other's checklist; substance-minus-empathy gap compared across languages within physician.
Key findings: Empathy did NOT separate safe from unsafe responses: AUC 0.49 (95% CI 0.39-0.62) - a coin flip. Within-physician slopes of empathy on safety and on substance were essentially zero (-0.006 and -0.004). Discriminant validity is clean: each dimension correlated strongly with its own checklist (r = 0.81 and 0.70) and essentially zero with the other's (0.01 and -0.01), so the two axes measure genuinely different constructs. CLINICAL SUBSTANCE FELL IN HEBREW - the substance-minus-empathy gap narrowed from 0.92 to 0.44 and from 1.09 to 0.52 in the two physicians who rated both languages, driven by LOWER SUBSTANCE rather than higher empathy. Ten responses were rated accurate yet unsafe. Unsafe ratings concentrated on the same three scenarios across all four products (p<0.001).
Soft spots: Preprint (v4). Two physicians per language is thin, and no inter-rater agreement statistic appears in the abstract - for a study whose central claims are rating-based that is the first thing to check in the full text. Twenty-one scenarios, forum-derived and clinician-adapted rather than live patient queries. Consumer chatbots rather than a clinician-facing deployed system, so population and risk profile differ from our kits. The Hebrew finding rests on two physicians - the right design for the question, but a small base.
Strengths: The within-physician estimation is what makes the empathy result credible. The discriminant-validity check is a step most evaluation papers skip entirely, and it converts 'empathy and safety are uncorrelated' from a null result into positive evidence that the instrument measures two distinct things. Keeping the English-Hebrew contrast within the same two physicians is the single best methodological decision in the paper. The scenario-concentration result reframes what to evaluate: risk clustered by SCENARIO, not by product.
Relevance: Layer 1: conversational / patient-facing systems; safety evaluation; rubric design; multi-dimensional scoring. Layer 2: (i) Score clinical content INDEPENDENTLY of empathy and never let one stand in for the other - belongs in the Rubric/Scoring guidance and the Dimension Reduction Principle, since collapsing dimensions is exactly what this shows you cannot do when they are orthogonal. Also sharpens safety_vs_effectiveness.md: empathy is an effectiveness-side dimension with ZERO safety information. (ii) Evaluate in each deployment language. Every live kit in this repository runs on Hebrew clinical text, and this is the FIRST source in the KB with direct physician-rated evidence that clinical substance degrades in Hebrew relative to English within the same raters - a strong argument that English-derived performance evidence is not transferable to the Hebrew kits. (iii) Anchor on high-risk scenarios rather than product comparison, since risk concentrated in the same three scenarios across all products.
Verdict reasoning: The most consequential find of the window for this repository. The design is careful in the specific ways that matter for the claims - within-physician, blinded, same-rater cross-language contrast, discriminant-validity check - and the central result, that the dimension patients can judge carries no information about the one that can hurt them, is both clean and directly actionable for rubric design. Import the design and the two rules; treat effect sizes as preprint-bound and check inter-rater statistics in the full text before citing the Hebrew magnitude numerically.

Williams R, Rutunda S, Nzabakira P, Mateen FJ. Human evaluators vs. LLM-as-a-Judge: toward scalable evaluation of GenAI in global health. npj Digital Medicine. 2026 Jul 20. doi:10.1038/s41746-026-02992-w. PMID 42477479.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-02992-w
Overview: A head-to-head test of the validated-LLM-judge pattern outside the setting where it was established. Five LLM judges and six human clinicians evaluated responses to questions actually posed by Rwandan health workers, across eleven evaluation criteria, in English and in Kinyarwanda. PEER-REVIEWED in npj Digital Medicine, which distinguishes it from most of the judge-validation evidence currently in the KB.
Eval question: Our existing judge guidance (KTP #8) is built almost entirely on English, high-resource, frontier-model evidence. Does the pattern hold when the language and the setting change - and does the standard mitigation, an LLM jury, actually fix the model-specific biases it is supposed to fix?
Methodology: Five LLM judges and six human clinicians independently evaluate the same responses. Eleven evaluation criteria assessed separately rather than collapsed into one score. LLM-jury construction tested explicitly as a mitigation for model-specific bias. English and Kinyarwanda conditions compared on both performance and cost-effectiveness.
Measurement: Per-criterion agreement between each LLM judge and the human evaluators; direction of miscalibration (lenient vs harsh) per model; jury-vs-single-judge agreement gain; cost-effectiveness by language.
Key findings: The best judge (Claude-4.1-Opus) matched human evaluators on ONLY 4 OF 11 CRITERIA. The failures were directional rather than random: Gemini-2.5-Pro scored systematically too leniently, GPT-5 systematically too harshly. Constructing LLM-juries to balance model-specific biases improved agreement on EXACTLY ONE additional criterion - 4 of 11 to 5 of 11. Performance AND cost-effectiveness both fell moving from English to Kinyarwanda. The authors conclude that inability to handle linguistic and cultural context is a critical limitation of the judge pattern.
Soft spots: Single country and single language pair, so 'linguistic and cultural context' is demonstrated rather than characterised - we learn that it breaks, not which property breaks it. Response count and per-criterion agreement statistics are not in the abstract. Six human evaluators with no human inter-rater band reported in the abstract, which matters because 'matched humans on 4 of 11 criteria' is only interpretable against how well the humans matched each other (the PQ-156 band-width caution applies). Criterion-level match is a coarse endpoint compared with a per-criterion kappa.
Strengths: Peer-reviewed, which is rare in this evidence pool. The PER-CRITERION framing is the methodological contribution - reporting that a judge matched humans on 4 of 11 criteria is far more useful than a single pooled agreement figure, because it forces the question of which criteria a given kit actually depends on. The NEGATIVE JURY RESULT is the most valuable single finding: LLM-juries are widely treated as the standard remedy for judge bias, and this is direct evidence that averaging across biased judges recovers very little when the biases are directional rather than noisy. Testing cost-effectiveness alongside accuracy is honest about the actual reason teams reach for LLM judges.
Relevance: Layer 1: LLM-as-judge validation; conversational clinical Q&A; multi-criteria rubric evaluation; non-English evaluation. Layer 2: extends KTP #8 in two directions the current evidence does not cover. (i) Validate the judge PER CRITERION, not in aggregate - a judge matching humans on 4 of 11 criteria is usable for a kit depending on those 4 and unusable for one depending on the other 7, and a pooled number cannot tell you which situation you are in. Composes with PQ-147 (Metric Match): choose which ITEMS to annotate, and now also which CRITERIA to validate on. (ii) The LLM-jury pattern is not a general fix for judge bias - our dual_evaluation_approach.md and the cross-family monitoring framework both lean on multi-judge designs, and this says the gain is small when biases are directional, which is exactly the self-preference/leniency structure PQ-149 and PQ-151-FU document. With PQ-159 and PQ-163 this is also a third independent line of evidence that evaluation instruments degrade outside English.
Verdict reasoning: Peer-reviewed, real-setting, and it lands two findings the KB currently lacks: per-criterion judge validation as the right granularity, and hard evidence against the jury-as-remedy assumption. Cite the 4-of-11 and the one-criterion jury gain as the mechanism; check the full text for the human inter-rater band before treating 'matched humans' as a strong bar.

Byun J, Lee S, Jung H, Jang J. Multi-Axial Analysis of Clinical Reasoning in Large Language Models: Inter-Verifier Disagreement and Its Implications for Automated Evaluation. Journal of Medical Systems. 2026 Jul 22. doi:10.1007/s10916-026-02440-y. PMID 42481841.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10916-026-02440-y
Overview: Attacks two assumptions at once: that reference-oriented semantic metrics tell you whether a stated diagnosis is supported by the model's own justification, and that independent verifier LLMs agree with each other. Three generator LLMs assessed on 1,000 MIMIC-IV hospital-stay cases along four axes - medical concept grounding, semantic similarity, semantic uncertainty, and evidence-conclusion coherence - with coherence judged independently by three frontier verifiers (Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5.4 mini). Peer-reviewed.
Eval question: If LLM-as-judge is the scaling plan for reasoning evaluation, how reproducible is the judgment? And does any existing metric actually check whether a model's rationale supports its own conclusion?
Methodology: Four complementary evaluation axes per case, with evidence-conclusion coherence as the novel one. Coherence judged INDEPENDENTLY BY THREE frontier verifiers, enabling a direct inter-verifier agreement measurement - the finding is only visible if you deliberately run three judges, and most studies run one. Preliminary human validation: a physician adjudicated 50 cases, with agreement computed separately per verifier. Analysis of a unanimous-agreement tier as a candidate for selective automation.
Measurement: Fleiss' kappa for inter-verifier agreement on coherence plus raw disagreement rate; per-axis scores; physician-vs-verifier agreement on the 50-case adjudicated subset.
Key findings: INTER-VERIFIER AGREEMENT ON COHERENCE IS CONSISTENTLY LOW: Fleiss' kappa 0.087-0.223, with 62.2%-74.3% disagreement. The same rationale is judged supported or unsupported depending on which verifier is asked. Separately, coherence exposes a dissociation the other axes miss: a model can score well on concept grounding and semantic similarity and still produce rationales that do not support its own stated conclusion. Physician adjudication of 50 cases echoed the spread - agreement with the physician varied across verifiers, so no single verifier stands in for clinical assessment. The unanimous-agreement tier (all three concur) is offered as a candidate for selective automation, with clinical reliability explicitly stated as unconfirmed.
Soft spots: The physician validation is 50 cases and a single adjudicator - the authors label it preliminary and it cannot settle which verifier is closest to clinical truth. Generators are 8B-70B rather than frontier, so the rationales being judged may be easier to fault than frontier output. Evidence-conclusion coherence is the authors' own construct with no external validation, and low inter-verifier agreement is at least partly attributable to CONSTRUCT AMBIGUITY rather than verifier unreliability - the paper cannot fully separate 'the judges are unreliable' from 'the question is ill-posed', which is the PB-08 task-well-posedness issue one level up. MIMIC-IV is English and US-centric.
Strengths: Running three verifiers instead of one is the whole contribution and a design choice most of the field skips. Reporting Fleiss' kappa rather than raw agreement is correct for three raters and makes the result comparable with the human-band material already in S1. The physician-adjudication arm, though small, keeps the study from being purely intrinsic. The unanimous-agreement tier is a constructive proposal rather than a bare negative - it points at a usable operating mode rather than just declaring the method broken.
Relevance: Layer 1: LLM-as-judge validation; clinical reasoning evaluation; agreement measurement; generation. Layer 2: this is the FOURTH independent confirmation of the rubric-vs-correctness pattern now stated as KTP #11, and it strengthens it in a new way - the previous three (PQ-150, PQ-151-FU, PQ-157) showed reasoning scores do not track accuracy; this shows the reasoning score is not even REPRODUCIBLE ACROSS JUDGES, with kappa an order of magnitude below any acceptable bar. Concretely: (i) KTP #8 should record that judge-to-judge reproducibility is a SEPARATE check from judge-vs-human agreement - a judge can agree with humans on average while three judges disagree with each other 70% of the time, and only the second check exposes it. (ii) The unanimous-agreement tier is directly portable to the cross-family LLM-judge monitoring framework - automate the unanimous cases, route the split ones to a human - and composes naturally with PQ-155 entropy triage. NOTE FOR THE POPC KIT, where a unanimous-Y agreement of 0.56 is already an open issue: this is external evidence that unanimity across independent judges is the defensible tier and split verdicts should route to code or to a human rather than being averaged. Also: HuatuoGPT-o1-8B, the medical-specialist model that approved 100% of failures as a judge in PQ-151-FU, appears here as a generator - a third data point in the domain-fine-tuning watch.
Verdict reasoning: Peer-reviewed, 1,000 real cases, and it answers a question the KB has been circling for four cycles: not 'is the judge right?' but 'is the judge STABLE?'. The kappa 0.087-0.223 figure is the single most citable number in this window for judge-validation guidance. Import the inter-verifier reproducibility check and the unanimous-agreement tier; treat the coherence construct itself as promising-but-unvalidated, and note honestly that construct ambiguity is a live alternative explanation for the low kappa.

Tyagi A, Taylor B, Jayaraman P, Jangda M, Jiang J, Kavula S, et al. Conversational multi-turn interaction does not ensure triage-disposition alignment in ChatGPT Health in real and synthetic patient encounters. medRxiv (preprint). 2026 Jul 23. doi:10.64898/2026.07.21.26358588.
https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.21.26358588
Overview: A retrospective cross-sectional evaluation of whether letting a consumer health assistant ASK FOLLOW-UP QUESTIONS improves its triage recommendations. 255 cases from three physician-reviewed sources - 39 clinically authored vignettes, 76 REAL emergency department cases, and 140 REAL nurse-line cases. Two conditions: single-turn (recommendation after only an initial symptom description, the realistic consumer pattern) and multi-turn (simulating nurse triage by asking follow-ups first). Mount Sinai / Icahn School of Medicine, 17 authors.
Eval question: Multi-turn interaction is widely assumed to improve clinical alignment because it gathers more information. Does it - and does aggregate agreement even capture what matters for a triage decision?
Methodology: Two prompting conditions on the same cases. Reference standards from two sources: nurse-line triage standards and clinician-adjudicated disposition. ERROR DIRECTION ANALYSED EXPLICITLY - discordant recommendations classified as over- or under-triage. Real and synthetic cases both included.
Measurement: Exact agreement with nurse-line standards; clinician-adjudicated disposition agreement; proportion of discordant cases representing under-triage with significance testing. The authors additionally argue for ordinal distance from standard and error direction as required reporting alongside aggregate agreement.
Key findings: Against nurse-line standards: 52.9% single-turn vs 55.7% multi-turn exact agreement - a small gain. But against CLINICIAN-ADJUDICATED disposition THE ORDERING REVERSES: 54.1% single-turn vs 48.2% multi-turn. Adding turns did not help and, on the better reference standard, hurt. In BOTH conditions roughly 70% of discordant recommendations were UNDER-TRIAGE (single-turn 70.8%, multi-turn 69.0%, both p<0.0001) - errors run overwhelmingly toward recommending less care than the standard. Absolute agreement sits in the low-to-mid 50s against either standard.
Soft spots: Preprint. One system, so this is not a model comparison and says nothing about generalisation. Retrospective and cross-sectional - the multi-turn arm SIMULATES nurse triage rather than observing real patients answering, so follow-up answers are constructed from the case record rather than produced by a person who might answer imprecisely. Nurse-line standards and clinician adjudication disagree with each other enough to FLIP the multi-turn result, and no inter-standard agreement figure appears in the abstract - worth checking in the full text, because the headline depends on which standard is treated as truth. No human-comparator arm attempting the same task under the same conditions.
Strengths: 216 of the 255 cases are REAL clinical encounters with an existing human disposition standard - a far better evidence base than the vignette studies that dominate this literature. Running two reference standards and reporting that they disagree is honest and is what makes the finding interesting rather than merely negative. The ERROR-DIRECTION analysis is the methodological contribution: an aggregate agreement of 55% is uninterpretable for safety purposes without knowing which way the 45% falls, and here it falls almost entirely toward under-triage - the direction that hurts patients.
Relevance: Layer 1: classification / triage / alerting; conversational and multi-turn systems; safety evaluation; ordinal outcomes. Layer 2: sharpens the PQ-152 depth axis promoted this cycle into something more specific and less intuitive. PQ-152 showed performance DEGRADES as conversational context accumulates; this shows that DELIBERATELY ADDING TURNS to gather more information does not recover it, and on the clinician-adjudicated standard made it worse. Together they say the trajectory axis is not a 'more context is better' story in either direction, and that a multi-turn deployment must be evaluated in its multi-turn form. Two concrete reporting imports: (i) report ORDINAL DISTANCE from the standard, not just exact agreement - for an ordinal outcome like triage disposition or the mRS (cf. PQ-158-FU), exact-match agreement discards the information that says how badly a miss missed; (ii) report ERROR DIRECTION as a first-class result and treat directional asymmetry as a safety finding, since a symmetric 45% error rate and a 45% error rate that is 70% under-triage are entirely different risk profiles. Both belong in the S1 classification guidance and apply directly to the MISSED CONSULTATION ALERT kit, where the alerting decision has exactly this shape.
Verdict reasoning: Real clinical data, a counter-intuitive and well-supported negative result, and - most usefully - a concrete reporting standard (ordinal distance + error direction) that generalises well beyond triage. Cite the design, the reversal between reference standards, and the under-triage asymmetry; treat the absolute agreement percentages as bound to this system and this institution's standards. Check the full text for an inter-standard agreement figure before leaning on the reversal.

Gabriel AO, Olawuyi D, Ajayi T, Aderemi T. Safety That Does Not Transfer: Cross-Lingual Clinical Correctness Drift in Deployable Medical Language Models. arXiv (preprint, v1). 2026 Jul 19. arXiv:2607.17270.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17270
Overview: Asks whether clinical safety established in English transfers to another language AT THE MODEL TIER THAT LOW-RESOURCE DEPLOYMENT ACTUALLY ADMITS - small quantised systems run locally, queried in local languages. Matched English-Hausa question pairs for three high-burden conditions in northern Nigeria (malaria, sickle cell disease, tuberculosis), probing knowledge recall, emergency triage, a leading question inviting a contraindicated action, and a traditional-remedy claim. Six models: five locally deployable 4-9B systems (two medically fine-tuned) plus one frontier system. All 128 responses scored against Nigerian national treatment guidelines by two fluent Hausa speakers working independently and blind to one another.
Eval question: Safety evaluation is done predominantly in English and predominantly on frontier systems. Neither condition describes deployment in a low-resource setting - so is English safety evidence portable, and if not, is the failure a property of the language, the clinical task, or the class of model?
Methodology: MATCHED English-Hausa question pairs so clinical content is held constant and only language varies. Four probe types per condition, deliberately including an adversarial-adjacent one and a cultural one. Six models spanning two tiers, with the frontier model serving as the ATTRIBUTION CONTROL. Two fluent Hausa speakers scoring independently and blind, against national treatment guidelines. Scoring on a signed scale where 2 = correct and -1 = actively harmful, so harm is representable rather than folded into 'incorrect'.
Measurement: Mean clinical correctness on a -1 to 2 scale; Cohen's kappa between the two raters, reported SEPARATELY for clinical correctness and for harm.
Key findings: Among locally deployable models, mean clinical correctness fell from 1.57 in English to -0.03 IN HAUSA - from broadly correct to, on average, no better than useless and bordering on harmful. The frontier model moved only 2.00 to 1.75 and produced NO response judged harmful in either language. Drift was consistent across all three clinical conditions, so it is not an artefact of one disease area. BECAUSE THE FRONTIER MODEL ANSWERS THE SAME QUESTIONS COMPETENTLY IN HAUSA, THE DEFICIT IS ATTRIBUTED TO THE DEPLOYABLE TIER - not to the language and not to the clinical material. Inter-rater agreement was substantial for clinical correctness (kappa = 0.70) but POOR FOR HARM (kappa = 0.22), and the authors examine that gap rather than burying it.
Soft spots: v1 preprint, 128 responses, two raters, one language pair, one country's guidelines. Two raters is thin for a rating endpoint, and the kappa of 0.22 on harm means the harm-related conclusions rest on a measurement the raters largely could not reproduce - the authors are transparent about this, but it bounds what can be claimed about harm specifically. Raters are Hausa-fluent but their clinical qualifications are not stated in the abstract, which matters when the reference standard is a national treatment guideline. Five deployable models is a small sample of a heterogeneous tier.
Strengths: The FRONTIER COMPARATOR is the design decision that matters - without it this would be one more 'model does worse in language X' observation; with it, the study separates a language effect from a model-tier effect. That is a genuine causal-attribution move on a small budget. Reporting kappa SEPARATELY for correctness and for harm is the second strength and arguably the more transferable: it surfaces that 'was this harmful?' is a much harder judgment for raters to reproduce than 'was this correct?'. Using a signed scale where harm is a negative value keeps the safety axis visible.
Relevance: Layer 1: conversational clinical Q&A; safety evaluation; non-English evaluation; model-selection criteria. Layer 2: (i) LANGUAGE IS AN EVALUATION AXIS and safety evidence is not portable across it - with PQ-159 (Hebrew) and PQ-160 (Kinyarwanda) this is the third independent finding in a single window, enough to state as guidance rather than observation. The refinement here is that the failure attached to the DEPLOYABLE MODEL TIER, which matters for any kit considering a locally-run open-weight model for privacy reasons - precisely the tradeoff PQ-155 makes attractive for the extraction kits. Running locally to keep data in-house and running in Hebrew are both reasonable choices; this says their INTERACTION needs its own evaluation. (ii) RATER AGREEMENT ON 'IS THIS HARMFUL' IS MUCH WORSE THAN ON 'IS THIS CORRECT' (kappa 0.22 vs 0.70) - a direct quantified warning for every safety rubric in this repository, where safety is always a hard gate. A hard gate resting on a judgment two raters reproduce at kappa 0.22 is not a gate; it argues for defining harm through explicit enumerable criteria (contraindicated action named, red-flag symptom missed) rather than as a holistic rating, and for measuring the harm-rating band before relying on it. Belongs in safety_vs_effectiveness.md; connects to PB-08 and the PQ-156 band-width caution.
Verdict reasoning: The volume is small and it is a v1 preprint, so no number here should be imported as a threshold. But the two transferable contributions are design-level and do not depend on sample size: the frontier-comparator attribution pattern (include a model tier you are not deploying, so you can tell a language effect from a tier effect) and the separate harm-vs-correctness agreement measurement. JMIR Med Educ. 2026 Jul 30. doi:10.2196/95578. PMID 42535722.
https://doi.org/10.2196/95578
Overview: A cross-sectional agreement study asking whether LLM-generated scores can substitute for faculty ratings, and — unusually — how much of the disagreement is attributable to the HUMAN raters rather than the model. 92 fourth-year medical students each completed one of three 15-minute voice-based virtual standardized patient cases (fever, diarrhea, cough). Eval question: Before concluding that an LLM judge is unreliable, how much of the apparent disagreement is actually the humans disagreeing with each other? This study answers it directly by partitioning variance, and reaches a conclusion about FIT FOR PURPOSE rather than a pass/fail on the model.
Methodology: • Cross-sectional agreement design with 10 BLINDED faculty raters — a genuine multi-rater human comparator rather than a single reference scorer • Three distinct cases, allowing case heterogeneity to be modelled rather than averaged away • Mixed-effects models to separate rater, case and residual variance • VARIANCE PARTITION COEFFICIENTS (VPC) to quantify how much residual variance is attributable to rater identity • Mixed-effects Bland-Altman analysis reporting mean bias AND limits-of-agreement WIDTH, plus a test for proportional bias • Agreement analysed separately for information gathering and communication, rather than pooled into one score • Pre-planned sensitivity analysis restricted to the lowest-performing quartile (the ceiling-effect check)
Measurement: A deliberately multi-statistic panel: ICC(2,1) for absolute agreement, Spearman ρ for rank correlation, mean absolute error (MAE), mixed-effects Bland-Altman for bias and limits of agreement, and VPC for variance attribution. Headline: ICC(2,1) = 0.51 (95% CI 0.34–0.65), MAE 3.11 points, mean bias 1.26 points (non-significant) but limits of agreement −4.95 to +7.48, WIDTH 12.43 points, with significant proportional bias (β_mean = −0.55, p<0.001). Rater variability accounted for 37% of residual variance in faculty scores (VPC = 0.37).
Key findings: • Median totals were nearly identical (AI 93.0 vs faculty 94.0) while absolute agreement was only moderate (ICC 0.51) — matching averages conceal per-student disagreement, a clean demonstration that aggregate equivalence is not agreement • RATER VARIABILITY WAS 37% of residual variance. More than a third of the disagreement is faculty disagreeing with faculty, so the achievable ceiling for any scorer is well below perfect • Agreement was substantially better for information gathering (ICC 0.54, VPC 0.23) than for communication (ICC 0.29, VPC 0.52) — and the domain where humans agreed least is exactly where the model agreed least.
