Philipp W, Fassbender F, Langer T, et al. Clinician-Level Agreement Without Clinical Caution: LLM Evaluator Limits in Medical AI Benchmarking. arXiv (preprint, v1). 2026 Jul 1. arXiv:2607.01103.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01103
Overview: Tests whether an LLM-as-judge can score OPEN-RESPONSE medical assessments the way physicians do — on accuracy AND appropriate caution. Builds MedQADE, a standardized German open-response clinical benchmark (3,800 items); responses scored by 10 practicing physicians + 9 LLM evaluators; LLM-vs-physician agreement compared against the physician-vs-physician ceiling. Task: LLM-as-judge / open-response scoring. Systems: 9 evaluators incl. Gemini 3 Flash. Signal: interpretive. Setting: German clinical exam items, 10 physician raters.
Eval question: Once an LLM-judge reaches clinician-level statistical agreement, is it safe to trust — or does agreement mask over-confidence, no abstention, and self-preference that a clinician rater would not exhibit?
Methodology: Open-response scoring of MedQADE by 10 physicians + 9 LLM evaluators; chance-corrected agreement (Cohen's κ) for LLM-vs-physician vs the physician ceiling; abstention-behavior and cross-model scoring-bias analysis.
Measurement: Cohen's κ vs physician ceiling; abstention rate on difficult items; self-preference (scores to architecturally similar vs dissimilar models).
Key findings: Top evaluator Gemini 3 Flash κ=0.694 vs physician ceiling κ=0.709 (statistically clinician-level, wide CIs). BUT physicians abstained on difficult items while frontier models scored every case → 'near-absent clinical metacognition.' Systematic self-preference bias toward architecturally similar models. Conclusion: statistical alignment does not ensure clinical caution.
Soft spots: Preprint; single German-language benchmark (US/Hebrew transfer unverified); wide CIs on the headline κ; self-preference measured only within this evaluator set.
Strengths: Directly tests the necessary-but-not-sufficient nature of the human-agreement-band test — a judge can sit inside the band yet lack abstention and carry self-preference bias. Real physician raters + abstention as an explicit axis (rare). Names a concrete failure mode (no metacognition) that band-membership hides.
Relevance: Layer 1: LLM-as-judge validation; open-response quality scoring. Layer 2: a direct caveat to KTP #8 and the human-inter-rater-band method (PQ-144/PQ-145) — reaching the physician κ band is necessary but not sufficient; also test abstention/metacognition and self-preference. Connects to PQ-131 (bias axis) and PQ-128 (judge order/position bias). If promoted: KTP #8 sub-point + cross-link from the band-test subsection.
Verdict reasoning: Strongest in-window find: plugs a real hole in the judge-validation/band-test guidance by showing statistical agreement can coexist with absent metacognition and self-preference bias. Preprint + single-language caveats → cite the mechanism and abstention/self-preference findings; treat the specific κ as illustrative.

Patel NA. Clinical Reasoning Graphs: Structured Evaluation of LLM Diagnostic Reasoning Reveals Competence Without Consistency. arXiv (preprint, v1). 2026 Jun 29. arXiv:2606.29876. (UCSF.)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29876
Overview: Asks whether LLMs that score 60–70% on diagnosis reason with stable, clinically-grounded schemas or just pattern-match. Diagnostic chain-of-thought traces are converted into clinical reasoning graphs (domain-grounded ontology: 5 node types, 7 edge types); tests whether clinically similar cases yield similar reasoning-graph structures. Task: diagnostic-reasoning / structural reasoning evaluation. Systems: 5 LLMs. Setting: 50 NEJM cases × 3 prompt conditions → 750 traces.
Eval question: Behind a respectable accuracy number, is an LLM's reasoning consistent and schema-stable across clinically similar cases, or is accuracy achieved without reproducible clinical logic?
Methodology: Extract clinical reasoning graphs from traces via a domain-grounded ontology; compare within-cluster vs between-cluster graph similarity for clinically similar cases; stratify similarity by correctness of model pairs.
Measurement: Graph-similarity scores; within- vs between-cluster comparison with multiple-comparison correction.
Key findings: Within-cluster and between-cluster graph similarity nearly equal, n.s. after correction — no case-specific schema stability. Graph similarity virtually identical whether model pairs both correct (0.488) or both incorrect (0.484). Interpretation: LLMs reach accuracy WITHOUT stable, schema-level reasoning — 'competence without consistency.'
Soft spots: Small case set (50 NEJM cases), 5 models, single author, preprint; bespoke ontology/similarity metric (cross-lab reproducibility unproven); NEJM cases are hard/atypical, may not generalize to routine reasoning.
Strengths: A genuinely different STRUCTURAL lens on reasoning consistency (graphs, not just answer variance). The correct-vs-incorrect similarity parity (0.488 vs 0.484) is a clean argument that accuracy ≠ reasoning stability. Complements the token/semantic consistency axis of PQ-141-FU.
Relevance: Layer 1: diagnostic-reasoning / generation evaluation; reasoning consistency. Layer 2: the reasoning-structure companion to PQ-141-FU (Shyr measures accuracy≠consistency on outputs; Patel on reasoning structure) and to PQ-91/PB-07 (stability). If promoted: an Extraction §2 / consistency-axis note that reasoning-schema stability is a third consistency lens beyond semantic and internal output variability — framework-only (small-N caveat).
Verdict reasoning: Provocative and timely — independently corroborates accuracy≠consistency from a reasoning-structure angle — but small-N, single-author, bespoke-ontology preprint. Promote the lens if wanted; hold the similarity numbers as illustrative.

Ajmal M, Roy A, Kanniyan AS, Kabeer JA, James J, Nakov P, Xie Z. CLExEval: A Human-in-the-Loop Framework for Qualitative Evaluation of LLM Clinical Reasoning. arXiv (preprint, v1). 2026 Jun 30. arXiv:2606.31608.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31608
Overview: A human-in-the-loop framework for evaluating clinical reasoning when fluent explanations can mask incorrect diagnoses (the 'evaluation illusion'). Combines expert-physician annotations with progressive information masking of reasoning traces, and separately measures how well an LLM-judge catches clinically incorrect outputs. Task: clinical-reasoning evaluation; LLM-as-judge reliability. Systems: incl. GPT-4o-mini (subject and evaluator) + a specialist model. Setting: 40 rare diagnostic cases; 200 traces; 5,600 expert annotations.
Eval question: Does an evaluation pipeline overestimate reliability because it rewards fluent-but-wrong reasoning — and can an LLM-judge be trusted to flag clinically incorrect outputs?
Methodology: Expert-physician annotation of 200 reasoning traces across 40 rare diagnostic cases (5,600 annotations); progressive information masking to test reasoning robustness under scarcity; LLM-as-evaluator reliability vs expert ground truth.
Measurement: Diagnostic accuracy under masking; reasoning-output mismatch rate; LLM-judge false-approval rate vs expert ground truth.
Key findings: GPT-4o-mini accuracy 95.0%→32.5% under information scarcity (verbosity bias). 68.6% reasoning-output mismatch (correct diagnosis in the reasoning but absent from the final answer). GPT-4o-mini as evaluator approved 47.9% of clinically incorrect outputs — quantifies the 'evaluation illusion.' A specialist model reached 92.5% max potential but failed reliable knowledge retrieval.
Soft spots: Preprint; 40 rare diagnostic cases — hard-case focus may overstate failure rates vs routine care; bespoke masking protocol (reproducibility unproven); dramatic accuracy drop is partly by construction (adversarial masking).
Strengths: Concrete KTP #8 anchor: a 47.9% LLM-judge false-approval rate on clinically incorrect outputs is a citable 'validate the judge' number. The 68.6% reasoning-output mismatch argues for scoring the final answer, not the reasoning narrative. Progressive-masking is a reusable robustness/adversarial protocol.
Relevance: Layer 1: LLM-as-judge validation; clinical-reasoning / generation evaluation; robustness. Layer 2: reinforces KTP #8 (validated-but-bounded judge — 47.9% false-approval quantifies the risk of an unvalidated judge) and PQ-111 (human-vs-judge discrimination); the masking protocol extends KTP #9 (adversarial/stress testing, cf. PQ-125-FU, PQ-77/PQ-59). If promoted: a KTP #8 false-approval anchor + a KTP #9 progressive-masking note.
Verdict reasoning: Strong, concrete failure-mode quantification from credible authors (Nakov), directly relevant to judge validation and reasoning robustness. Preprint + rare-case-only caveats → verify-grade, but the 47.9% judge false-approval and 68.6% reasoning-output-mismatch numbers are the keepers if promoted.
